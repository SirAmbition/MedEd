# 01 Extract: T1DM in children and adolescents (epidemiology, presentation, diagnosis)

Paraphrased key points with source page (p.N of the 15-page PDF). Glucose in mmol/L (the source gives mg/dL first). Flags: **[PEARL]** = likely new to a PGY1; **[BASIC]** = a PGY1 already knows this, use only as context; **[THRESHOLD]** = number the owner must check manually.

## A. Big picture
- T1DM results from insulin deficiency after beta cell destruction. A quarter of all T1DM is diagnosed in adults. [PEARL] (p.1)
- Still the commonest diabetes of childhood despite rising youth T2DM. [BASIC] (p.1)
- About 30% of children present in DKA. [THRESHOLD] (p.2)
- Screening of first-degree relatives is identifying more children at presymptomatic stages. (p.2)

## B. Epidemiology
- Worldwide mean incidence about 14 per 100,000 per year (age 0 to 20). Highest in Finland and Sardinia, 45 to 65 per 100,000 (under 15). (p.2)
- US: 22.3 per 100,000 per year; higher in non-Hispanic White youth than Black or Hispanic youth. US youth prevalence 3.5 per 1000. (p.2)
- Onset is bimodal: one peak at 4 to 6 years, a second at 10 to 14 years (early puberty). About 45% present before age 10. [PEARL] (p.2)
- Later peak at 15 to 19 years in Middle Eastern and African countries. (p.2)
- In US youth, T1DM is nearly all diabetes under 10, 90% at 10 to 14, 80% at 15 to 19. Summary on p.13 gives about 80% for 10 to 19 combined (source variance). (p.2 to 3, p.13)
- After puberty, incidence falls in females but stays relatively high in males until the late 20s. [PEARL] (p.3)
- No overall sex difference in childhood (unusual for an autoimmune disease); male excess of about 1.5:1 in young adults. [PEARL] (p.3)
- Incidence rising: 0.3% per year globally (1990 to 2017); 2 to 5% per year in Europe, Middle East and **Australia**; about 2% per year in the US. Cause unknown. (p.3)

## C. Risk by family history (lifetime risk of T1DM)
- No family history: 0.4% (p.3)
- Affected mother: 1 to 4%. Affected father: 3 to 8%. **Father carries higher risk than mother.** [PEARL] (p.3)
- Both parents: reported up to 30% (p.4)
- Non-twin sibling: 3 to 6% by age 20; 10% by 60 (p.4)
- Dizygotic twin: 8%. Monozygotic twin: 30% within 10 years of the first twin's diagnosis; 65% concordance by age 60. [PEARL] (p.4)
- Heritability is mostly MHC (HLA) region polymorphisms. Genetic risk scores proposed (to separate T1 from T2 or monogenic, and to target prevention) but not yet validated, especially outside European ancestry. (p.4)
- Environmental triggers (viruses, foods) suspected; none proven. (p.4, p.14)

## D. Staging [PEARL]
- Stage 1: 2 or more islet autoantibodies, normoglycaemia, no symptoms. (p.5)
- Stage 2: 2 or more autoantibodies plus dysglycaemia (abnormal glucose tolerance), usually no symptoms. (p.5)
- Stage 3: glucose above diagnostic thresholds, usually symptomatic. (p.5)
- Stage 4: established, long-standing disease. (p.5)
- Screen-detected children are staged with ISPAD criteria; the source notes autoantibody screening of relatives is not a clinical care recommendation. (p.6 to 7)

## E. Presentation
Three routes: classic new onset (commonest), DKA (second), silent screen-detected (least common). (p.4 to 6)

Classic onset (p.5)
- Polyuria starts once glucose exceeds the renal threshold, about 10 mmol/L. [THRESHOLD] Presents as nocturia, enuresis, new daytime wetting, or heavier and more frequent wet nappies. [PEARL]
- Thirst from raised serum osmolality and hypovolaemia. Children may be hypovolaemic yet **not look dry** (normal mucosa and turgor). [PEARL]
- Weight loss from hypovolaemia plus catabolism; appetite rises first, then ketosis brings nausea and anorexia.
- Polyuria and polydipsia in over 90%, but often only found by asking directly. Weight loss in about half. Mean symptom duration before diagnosis about 10 days (Irish study). [PEARL]

Other features (p.6)
- Perineal candidiasis, especially in young children and girls. [PEARL]
- Acute blurred vision from osmotic change in the lens.
- Cataract occasionally (0.25% in a large cohort; more in females, higher HbA1c, older onset).

DKA (p.6)
- Fruity breath, drowsiness, lethargy on top of classic symptoms.
- **Mimics a vomiting illness**: the usual dehydration sign (reduced urine output) is hidden by glycosuric polyuria. [PEARL]
- DKA at diagnosis about 30% (range 15 to 70%). More likely under 6 years or with socioeconomic disadvantage; **over half of children under 3 present in DKA**. [PEARL] [THRESHOLD]

Young children under 6 (p.7, p.14)
- Dehydrate faster (cannot self-increase intake); diabetes less often suspected by families and clinicians, so longer illness and worse decompensation.
- Polyuria and thirst hard to detect in nappies or a non-verbal child, especially under 2.
- **Prolonged or recurrent nappy-area candidiasis** should prompt a glucose check; it was present at diagnosis in a significant proportion of under 6s, especially under 2s. [PEARL]
- These children have often already seen a clinician for vague complaints. With dehydration, abdominal pain or fatigue, check glucose and urine glucose. [PEARL]

Infants (p.7, p.12)
- Neonatal diabetes is rarely, if ever, autoimmune: it is monogenic (often K-ATP channel subunit genes); most are small for gestational age. [PEARL]

## F. Diagnosis (p.8, p.14)
Any one of: [THRESHOLD]
- Fasting plasma glucose 7.0 mmol/L or more (fast of at least 8 hours), on more than one occasion.
- Random venous plasma glucose 11.1 mmol/L or more **with classic symptoms**.
- 2-hour OGTT glucose 11.1 mmol/L or more; load 1.75 g/kg, max 75 g. Rarely needed in children (most are symptomatic and well above threshold).
- HbA1c 6.5% or more (NGSP-certified assay), which in children must be confirmed by another measure of hyperglycaemia.
- Without unequivocal symptomatic hyperglycaemia, repeat testing confirms the diagnosis.

HbA1c traps (p.8)
- Reflects about the previous 10 to 12 weeks.
- German data: all symptomatic new T1DM had HbA1c 6.35% or more; transient (stress) hyperglycaemia gave 4.5 to 6.1%. [PEARL] [THRESHOLD]
- Misleading with haemoglobin variants or rapid red cell turnover; boronate affinity assays resist variants but not rapid turnover. [PEARL]

## G. Type 1 vs type 2 (p.8 to 10)
- No single test or criteria set separates them; combine phenotype, history and labs.
- Habitus: T2DM usually BMI 95th centile or more; **up to 25% of T1DM are overweight** (85th to 95th centile); new T2DM can also lose weight. [PEARL]
- Age: T2DM after puberty, almost never under 10.
- Insulin resistance signs: acanthosis in 50 to 90% of US youth with T2DM, but also about 12% of T1DM; up to 25% of T1DM have biochemical insulin resistance. [PEARL]
- Family history: up to 10% of T1DM vs 75 to 90% of T2DM have an affected close relative. [PEARL]
- Autoantibodies: GAD65, IA-2, insulin (IAA), ZnT8. Send at presentation, **before or soon after starting insulin**: insulin antibodies can appear 10 to 14 days after exogenous insulin. [PEARL] [THRESHOLD]
- Antibody positive = type 1A. Clinical T1DM with no antibodies and no other cause = type 1B. **Up to 30% of clinical T2DM phenotype are antibody positive** (slowly progressive autoimmune diabetes). [PEARL]
- Insulin and C-peptide: measure when T2DM is possible, but **only after recovery** from the acute presentation: glucotoxicity and illness suppress them. T1DM: inappropriately low for the glucose; T2DM: high. [PEARL]
- US registry phenotypes: autoimmune and insulin sensitive 55%; non-autoimmune and insulin resistant 16%; **both autoimmunity and insulin resistance 20%** (obesity with T1DM); neither 10% (mostly labelled T1 or T2; consider monogenic diabetes). [PEARL]

## H. Differential (p.10 to 12)
- In a previously well child, diabetes is by far the commonest cause of significant hyperglycaemia. (p.10)
- Non-diabetic hyperglycaemia: critical illness and septic shock; IV dextrose, acute sympathomimetics, high-dose glucocorticoids (resolves when stopped); neonatal causes (excess glucose infusion, prematurity, stress, sepsis, drugs). (p.10 to 11)
- Monogenic diabetes (formerly MODY): non-insulin-dependent diabetes before 25, autosomal dominant through 3 generations, antibody negative, no acanthosis. Confirm by gene sequencing. [PEARL] (p.11)
- Exocrine pancreas: cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis, chronic pancreatitis. (p.11)
- Endocrine: Cushing syndrome, GH excess, glucagonoma, phaeochromocytoma (all but Cushing extremely rare). Paediatric Cushing clue: **falling height velocity despite weight gain**; hyperglycaemia is very rare in childhood Cushing though common in adults. Tests: 24-hour urinary free cortisol, late-night salivary cortisol (2 or more), overnight low-dose dexamethasone suppression; 2 of 3 positive. [PEARL] (p.11 to 12)
- Drugs: glucocorticoids, HIV protease inhibitors, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, L-asparaginase, atypical antipsychotics; immune checkpoint inhibitors cause insulin-deficient diabetes. (p.12)
- Glycosuria with normal plasma glucose: renal glycosuria or Fanconi syndrome. (p.12)
