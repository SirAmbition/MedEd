## Post 1: post-01_approach (Monday, 10 slides)

**Title:** New diabetes in a child: the work-up in order
**Series:** Approach
**Angle:** A PGY1 faced with a thirsty, wetting child needs the order of decisions: which single result counts, what HbA1c can and cannot tell them, and when a sample drawn at the wrong time misleads. Save it for the next new-onset child on the ward.

| Slide | Purpose | Specific carried | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Hook | Counter-intuitive claim | Symptomatic new T1DM can sit just under the 6.5% cut-off. German data: HbA1c 6.35% or more in all symptomatic new cases (single cohort, flag as such). Headline idea: "Symptomatic new T1DM can sit just under 6.5%" | p.8 |
| 2. Trigger | Who is easy to miss | Ask directly about nocturia, night wetting, new daytime wetting, heavier wet nappies. Polyuria and polydipsia in over 90% but often found only by asking. Two age peaks: 4 to 6 years and 10 to 14 years, about 45% before age 10 | p.5, p.2 |
| 3. Risk stratify | What changes urgency | DKA at diagnosis about 30% (range 15 to 70%). More likely under 6 or with socioeconomic disadvantage. Over half of children under 3 present in DKA. Headline idea: "Under-3s: over half arrive in DKA" | p.6 |
| 4. Immediate decision | Confirm now or repeat | Random venous glucose 11.1 mmol/L or more with classic symptoms is diagnostic on one reading. Without unequivocal symptoms, repeat testing is needed. Fasting (at least 8 h) 7.0 mmol/L or more on more than one occasion. **Gap:** the extract has no urgency or acute management content (DKA grading and fluids are off limits), so this slide carries the diagnostic decision only | p.8, p.14 |
| 5. Test 1: HbA1c | Key test and its limits | HbA1c 6.5% or more (NGSP-certified) must be confirmed by another measure of hyperglycaemia in children. Reflects the previous 10 to 12 weeks. Haemoglobin variants and rapid red-cell turnover mislead; boronate affinity assays resist variants but not rapid turnover. Headline idea: "HbA1c is a 12-week average, not a glucose" | p.8, p.14 |
| 6. Test 2: antibodies | How to type it properly | GAD65, IA-2, IAA and ZnT8 sent at presentation, before or soon after insulin. Insulin antibodies can appear 10 to 14 days after exogenous insulin, so an IAA result drawn after insulin cannot be read as evidence of autoimmunity (inference from the extract, flag for GM A). Headline idea: "Draw antibodies before the first insulin dose" | p.8 to 10 |
| 7. Interpreting results | What changes management and the false reassurance | C-peptide and insulin only after recovery. T1DM: inappropriately low for the glucose. T2DM: high. Glucotoxicity and acute illness suppress both, so a sick-child sample in T2DM can look like T1DM (inference, flag). Clinical T1DM with no antibodies and no other cause is type 1B, so antibody-negative does not mean T2DM. Headline idea: "A low C-peptide in acute illness can mislead" | p.8 to 10 |
| 8. Cause and next step | Non-diabetic and drug causes | Non-diabetic hyperglycaemia: critical illness, IV dextrose, acute sympathomimetics, high-dose glucocorticoids (resolves when stopped), neonatal causes. Drug list: glucocorticoids, HIV protease inhibitors, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, L-asparaginase, atypical antipsychotics. Headline idea: "Steroids raise glucose, and it often resolves when stopped" | p.10 to 12 |
| 9. Registrar pearl | The mistake interns make | Glycosuria with normal plasma glucose is renal glycosuria or Fanconi syndrome, not diabetes. Check plasma glucose before reading any urine glucose as diabetes. Headline idea: "Glycosuria with normal glucose is not diabetes" | p.12 |
| 10. Summary | Sequence and save CTA | One line: glucose decision, HbA1c with its limits, antibodies before insulin, C-peptide after recovery, then the cause. Save for the next new-onset child | p.8 to 14 |

**Level check:** Slides 1, 5, 6, 7 and 9 teach things a PGY1 is unlikely to hold reliably. Slides 2 and 4 are partly basic (classic symptoms, random glucose rule).

## Post 3: post-03_friday-case (Friday, 5 slides)

**Title:** Fictional case: wet nappies and a vomiting toddler
**Series:** Friday Case
**Angle:** The usual dehydration sign (falling urine output) is hidden by glycosuric polyuria, so a vomiting toddler with a "gastro" label can be in ketosis. One clue decides it.

| Slide | Purpose | Specific carried | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Vignette | Real-looking data, "Fictional case" label | Fictional case. 2-year-old girl, 4 days of vomiting and reduced intake, labelled gastroenteritis at a GP visit on day 2. Mum reports nappies heavier and more frequent than usual, recurrent nappy candidiasis for about 2 months, treated twice with topical antifungal. Obs: HR 132/min, RR 32/min, temperature 37.2 C, capillary refill 2 s, mucous membranes moist. Weight 12.4 kg (13.0 kg at 2-year check). No affected relative documented. No steroid use | p.6, p.7, p.14 |
| 2. Question | The one question | Most likely diagnosis? | n/a |
| 3. Options | Five plausible options | A. Viral gastroenteritis with dehydration. B. Recurrent nappy candidiasis alone, treat and review. C. Cushing syndrome. D. Early T1DM with ketosis. E. Monogenic diabetes | p.6, p.7, p.11 to 12 |
| 4. Answer and clue | One discriminating clue | D. The clue is urine output: wet nappies are heavier and more frequent despite four days of vomiting. In the usual dehydrated child urine falls. Glycosuric polyuria masks that sign | p.6 |
| 5. Why each is wrong and teaching point | Distractor logic and the pearl | A: the vomiting label is the mimic (p.6). B: candidiasis is a prompt to check glucose, not a cause (p.6 to 7). C: needs falling height velocity despite weight gain; height is stable here (p.11 to 12). E: monogenic presents before 25 and usually with an affected family member and no antibodies; none is documented here (p.11). Teaching point: under-6s dehydrate faster and are diagnosed late (p.7, p.14) | p.6, p.7, p.11 to 12, p.14 |

**Level check:** The "urine output preserved in a vomiting child" mechanism is the pearl most PGY1s miss. Candidiasis as a glucose prompt is partly known.

**Discrimination notes:** The answer hinges on one clue (urine output). Distractor C (Cushing) is defensible only for a child with weight gain; the vignette keeps height stable so it fails on the clue. Distractor E (monogenic) is the weakest, because a 2-year-old with no family history is an unusual choice; it is kept for the discrimination of the family pattern.

|---|---|---|
| Level | 4 | Glycosuric polyuria masking the dehydration sign is new to most PGY1s |
| Discrimination | 3 | One clue decides it, but distractor E is weak at age 2 and distractor B partly overlaps the clue |
| Specificity | 4 | Obs and weight given; the answer clue is precise; no DKA grading needed |

## Post 4: post-04_side-by-side (Sunday, 6 slides)

**Title:** Youth T1DM, T2DM and monogenic: where they overlap
**Series:** Side by Side
**Angle:** The features a PGY1 uses to type youth diabetes overlap more than they separate, so a single feature misleads. Compare the rows that discriminate and say plainly when a row does not.

| Slide | Purpose | Specific carried | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Frame | Set the rule | No single test or criteria set separates T1DM from T2DM. Combine phenotype, history and labs. Headline idea: "No single test separates youth diabetes types" | p.8 to 10 |
| 2. Habitus (overlap row) | Weight does not discriminate | T2DM usually BMI 95th centile or more. Up to 25% of T1DM are overweight (85th to 95th centile). New T2DM can lose weight. Row shows overlap, not a separator. Headline idea: "Overweight does not settle T1 or T2" | p.8 to 10 |
| 3. Antibodies (overlap row) | The T2DM antibody surprise | Antibody-positive T1A vs type 1B. Up to 30% of the clinical T2DM phenotype are antibody positive (slowly progressive autoimmune diabetes). Headline idea: "A third of clinical T2DM can be antibody-positive" | p.8 to 10 |
| 4. Insulin resistance (overlap row) | The four-way registry split | Acanthosis in 50 to 90% of US youth with T2DM but about 12% of T1DM. Up to 25% of T1DM have biochemical insulin resistance. US registry: autoimmune and insulin sensitive 55%; non-autoimmune and insulin resistant 16%; both autoimmunity and insulin resistance 20%. Headline idea: "One in five youth T1DM has both phenotypes" | p.8 to 10 |
| 5. Family history (strong row) | The discriminating feature | Affected close relative: up to 10% of T1DM vs 75 to 90% of T2DM. Headline idea: "Family history separates better than weight" | p.8 to 10 |
| 6. Monogenic and close | When neither fits | Monogenic: non-insulin-dependent diabetes before 25, autosomal dominant over 3 generations, antibody negative, no acanthosis. The 10% "neither" registry group should prompt monogenic testing. Confirm by gene sequencing. Close: "Antibody-negative, no acanthosis, family pattern: send for genes." Save CTA | p.8 to 11 |

**Level check:** Family history percentages and acanthosis are familiar to a PGY1. The registry split, antibody-positive T2DM phenotype and the monogenic criteria are new.

**Discrimination notes:** Slides 2 and 4 are overlap rows and do not separate the conditions; they are kept on purpose because the brief asks for the overlaps, and each says so. Slides 3, 5 and 6 separate.

**Self-score (Side by Side rubric):**

| Line | Score | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Level | 3 | Family history and acanthosis are known; the registry split, antibody-positive T2DM and monogenic criteria are new |
| Discrimination | 3 | Strong rows on family history and monogenic criteria; two of six rows are overlap rows and do not separate by design |
| Specificity | 5 | Numbers or criteria on every row |
| Sourcing | 3 | All traceable to p.8 to 11; the registry figures are single-cohort and need the open-guideline check |
