## Post 1: post-01_approach (Monday, 10 slides)

**Title:** New diabetes in a child: the work-up in order
**Series:** Approach
**Angle:** A PGY1 facing a thirsty, wetting child needs the order of decisions: which single result is enough today, what to send before insulin, and which sample taken at the wrong time misleads.

| Slide | Purpose | Specific carried | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Hook | Counter-intuitive, decision-first | Classic symptoms plus a random venous glucose of 11.1 mmol/L or more is diagnostic today: no OGTT, no repeat. Headline: "Symptoms plus random 11.1 mmol/L: diagnosed today" | p.8 |
| 2. Trigger | Less-known misses | Polyuria and polydipsia in over 90% but mostly found only by asking directly. Weight loss in about half. Mean symptom duration about 10 days in one Irish study. A child can be hypovolaemic yet not look dry (normal mucosa and turgor). Ask about nocturia, bedwetting and daytime incontinence. Two age peaks: 4 to 6 years and 10 to 14 years. About 45% present before age 10 | p.5 (lines on Irish study, 90%, half, mucosa), p.2 (peaks), p.9 (45%) |
| 3. Risk stratify | What changes urgency | DKA at diagnosis about 30%, range 15 to 70%. More likely under 6 or with socioeconomic disadvantage. More than half of children under 3 present in DKA. Headline: "Under-3s: over half arrive in DKA" | p.6 |
| 4. Immediate decision | Send before insulin | GAD65, IA-2, IAA and ZnT8 at presentation, before or soon after starting insulin. Insulin antibodies may develop about 10 to 14 days after exposure to exogenous insulin, so the source's advice is to sample at presentation or soon after starting insulin. Headline: "Draw antibodies before the first insulin dose" | p.9 |
| 5. Test 1: criteria and HbA1c | Confirming diabetes and the HbA1c limit | Fasting glucose 7.0 mmol/L or more on more than one occasion (fast at least 8 h). OGTT seldom necessary. HbA1c 6.5% or more (NGSP-certified assay) must be confirmed by another measure of hyperglycaemia. HbA1c reflects about 10 to 12 weeks. Haemoglobin variants and rapid red-cell turnover mislead; boronate affinity assays resist variants but not turnover. Headline: "HbA1c is a 12-week average, not a glucose" | p.8 |
| 6. Test 2: typing | Type 1A and 1B | Antibody positive is type 1A. Clinical T1DM with no detectable autoantibodies is type 1B. Headline: "Antibody-negative T1DM is still type 1B" | p.10 |
| 7. Interpreting results | C-peptide timing | Measure insulin and C-peptide only after recovery from the initial hyperglycaemic stress, because glucotoxicity and acute illness suppress them. T1DM: inappropriately low for the glucose. T2DM: high. Headline: "C-peptide is only useful after recovery" | p.10 |
| 8. Cause and next step | Non-diabetic and drug causes | In a previously healthy child, diabetes is by far the most common cause of clinically significant hyperglycaemia. Other causes: critically ill patients and septic shock; high-dose glucocorticoids and acute sympathomimetic agents. Drug-induced diabetes: glucocorticoids, HIV protease inhibitors, ciclosporin, L-asparaginase, tacrolimus, atypical antipsychotics. Headline: "Steroids and critical illness can raise glucose" | p.10 (common cause, critical illness), p.11 (steroids, sympathomimetics), p.12 (drug list) |
| 9. Registrar pearl | The mistake interns make | Glycosuria with normal plasma glucose is renal glucosuria or Fanconi syndrome, not diabetes. Check plasma glucose before reading urine glucose as diabetes. Headline: "Glycosuria with normal glucose is not diabetes" | p.12 |
| 10. Summary | Sequence and save CTA | One line: glucose decision, antibodies before insulin, HbA1c limits, type 1A or 1B, C-peptide after recovery, then the cause. Save for the next new-onset child | p.8, p.9, p.10, p.12 |

**Level check:** Slides 1, 4, 6, 7 and 9 teach things a PGY1 is unlikely to hold reliably. Slides 2 and 8 are partly known; slide 2 is kept to the less-known misses.

## Post 3: post-03_friday-case (Friday, 5 slides)

**Title:** Fictional case: wet nappies and a vomiting toddler
**Series:** Friday Case
**Angle:** Glycosuric polyuria keeps urine output up, which hides the dehydration sign a vomiting child would normally show. A "gastro" label can sit on a child who is in ketosis.

| Slide | Purpose | Specific carried | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Vignette | Real-looking data, "Fictional case" label | Fictional case. 2-year-old girl, 4 days of vomiting and reduced intake, labelled acute vomiting illness at a GP visit on day 2. Mum says nappies are heavier and more frequent than usual. Recurrent nappy candidiasis for about 2 months, treated twice with topical antifungal. Obs: HR 132/min, RR 32/min, temperature 37.2 C, capillary refill 2 s. Weight 12.4 kg (13.0 kg at 2-year check). Urine dipstick: glucose positive, ketones positive. Plasma glucose not yet reported. No steroid, dextrose or critical illness. No affected relative documented | p.6, p.7 |
| 2. Question | The one question | Most likely diagnosis? | n/a |
| 3. Options | Five plausible options | A. Acute vomiting illness (gastroenteritis). B. Recurrent nappy candidiasis alone, treat and review. C. Cushing syndrome. D. Early T1DM with ketosis. E. Renal glycosuria or Fanconi syndrome | p.6, p.7, p.11 to 12, p.12 |
| 4. Answer and clue | One discriminating clue | D. The clue: urine output is preserved (heavier, more frequent wet nappies) despite four days of vomiting. This defeats A, where urine output should fall. It also defeats B, which does not explain the vomiting and weight loss. C is defeated by stable height. E is defeated only by a plasma glucose result, the next check (see slide 5). | p.6, p.12 |
| 5. Why each is wrong and teaching point | Distractor logic and the pearl | A: the vomiting label is the mimic; glycosuric polyuria masks the usual falling urine output in dehydration (p.6). B: prolonged or recurrent nappy candidiasis should prompt a glucose check, not treatment alone (p.7, p.14). C: Cushing presents with growth velocity falling despite weight gain; height is stable here (p.11 to 12). ## Post 4: post-04_side-by-side (Sunday, 6 slides)

**Title:** Youth T1DM, T2DM and monogenic: where they overlap
**Series:** Side by Side
**Angle:** The features a PGY1 uses to type youth diabetes overlap more than they separate. Each slide says whether the row separates the types or overlaps them.

| Slide | Purpose | Specific carried | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Frame | Set the rule | No set of criteria or diagnostic test can consistently distinguish T1DM from T2DM. Headline: "No single test separates youth diabetes types" | p.9 |
| 2. Habitus and age | Weight overlaps; age separates | T2DM usually BMI 95th centile or more. Up to 25% of T1DM overweight (85th to 95th centile). New-onset T2DM can have recent weight loss. Age separates: about 45% of T1DM present before age 10, and almost all T2DM presents after age 10. Verdict: weight overlaps, age separates. Headline: "Weight overlaps; age under 10 does not" | p.9 |
| 3. Antibodies (overlap) | The T2DM antibody surprise | Up to 30% of individuals with the clinical T2DM phenotype are antibody positive, a slowly progressive autoimmune diabetes. Verdict: overlap. Headline: "Up to 30% of clinical T2DM is antibody positive" | p.10 |
| 4. Insulin resistance (separator) | Acanthosis separates, with a gap | Acanthosis nigricans in 50 to 90% of US youth with T2DM, against about 12% of those with T1DM. Up to 25% of clinically diagnosed T1DM have biochemical insulin resistance. Verdict: acanthosis separates, but not absolutely. Headline: "Acanthosis separates, but 1 in 8 T1DM has it" | p.9 |
| 5. Registry (overlap) | The four-way registry split | In a US youth registry: 55% autoimmune and insulin sensitive (T1DM); 16% non-autoimmune and insulin resistant (T2DM); 20% both autoimmunity and insulin resistance, a pattern typical of obesity with T1DM; 10% insulin sensitive without islet autoimmunity. The 20% is a share of this cohort, not of T1DM. Verdict: overlap. Headline: "One in five youth in a US registry had both" | p.10 |
| 6. Family history and monogenic (separators) | The strongest separator and the rare group | Affected close relative: up to 10% of T1DM vs 75 to 90% of T2DM (separates). Consider monogenic diabetes for the 10% insulin-sensitive, antibody-negative group (p.10). Monogenic criteria: under 25 years, non-insulin-dependent, autosomal dominant across three generations, no islet autoantibodies, no acanthosis; confirm by gene sequencing (p.11). Verdict: family history separates; monogenic is a consider-and-test pathway. Close: "Antibody-negative, no acanthosis, family pattern: consider genes." Save CTA | p.9, p.10, p.11 |

**Level check:** Family history and acanthosis are known to many PGY1s. The 30% antibody-positive T2DM figure, the 20% both-phenotype registry split and the monogenic criteria are new.

**Discrimination notes:** Four of six rows separate (age, acanthosis, family history, monogenic). Two overlap rows (weight and antibody-positive T2DM, plus the registry split) are labelled as overlap by design.
