Slide 1: Symptoms plus random 11.1 mmol/L: diagnosed today. Classic symptoms plus a random venous glucose of 11.1 mmol/L or more diagnose diabetes. If symptoms are not unequivocal, confirm by repeat testing. An OGTT is seldom needed. Visual: A clean glass card carrying the bold glucose figure, beside a symptom checklist icon.

Slide 2: Ask about night wetting, not just thirst. Over 90% have polyuria and polydipsia, yet these often surface only when you ask about wet nappies, night wetting, new daytime wetting or thirst. The child may be hypovolaemic without looking dry. Visual: A question mark sits above a bed icon, with a small water-drop icon beside it.

Slide 3: Under-3s: over half arrive in DKA. DKA is the first presentation in about 30% of children, range 15 to 70%. Risk is higher under 6 years and with socioeconomic disadvantage. Over half of under-3s present this way. Visual: A vertical age ladder with the under-3 rung highlighted in deep teal.

Slide 4: Draw antibodies before the first insulin dose. Send ZnT8, GAD65, IAA and IA-2 at presentation, ideally before the first insulin dose or soon after. Insulin antibodies may appear about 10 to 14 days after insulin is first given. Visual: A syringe sits beside four sample tubes, with a clock icon showing timing.

Slide 5: HbA1c looks back 10 to 12 weeks. Confirm HbA1c of 6.5% or more with a second measure. Fasting glucose of 7.0 mmol/L or more on two occasions also counts. Haemoglobin variants and rapid red-cell turnover mislead HbA1c. Visual: A calendar strip spanning about three months, with a falling glucose line across it.

Slide 6: Antibody-negative T1DM is still type 1B. Most T1DM shows islet antibodies, the type 1A pattern. Clinical T1DM with no detectable antibodies and no other cause is type 1B. Visual: A two-branch tree: the left branch is antibody positive, the right branch is antibody negative.

Slide 7: Measure C-peptide once the child has recovered. If T2DM is possible, measure insulin and C-peptide after recovery from the hyperglycaemic stress. Glucotoxicity and acute illness can suppress both. Low for the glucose points to T1DM; high points to T2DM. Visual: A timeline with an acute-illness block, then a recovery arrow leading to a lab tube.

Slide 8: Steroids and critical illness can raise glucose. Diabetes is the main cause of significant hyperglycaemia in a previously well child. Non-diabetic causes: critical illness, high-dose steroids, acute sympathomimetics. Drug-induced diabetes can follow atypical antipsychotics, tacrolimus or glucocorticoids. Visual: Three drug-pill icons stacked beside a magnifier over a glucose line.

Slide 9: Glycosuria with normal glucose is not diabetes. Renal glycosuria and Fanconi syndrome both spill glucose into urine while plasma glucose stays normal. Check plasma glucose before reading urine glucose as diabetes. Visual: A urine dipstick with a question mark over the glucose pad.

Slide 10: Glucose, antibodies, HbA1c, C-peptide, then cause. Working order, not a protocol: symptoms plus glucose 11.1 mmol/L or more; antibodies before insulin; HbA1c limits; type 1A or 1B; C-peptide after recovery; then non-diabetic and drug causes. Save for your next new-onset child. Visual: Six stepped blocks rising like a staircase, with a save bookmark at the top.
